gamedesign.uk is about the practice of game design as it is actually done inside working studios: how a team gets from an idea to a shipped system, who decides what, how balance work is reviewed and signed off, how design documentation is kept alive rather than written once and abandoned, how the pipeline between design, engineering and art is run day to day, and how design roles are hired for, grown and led. It is written for people who already do this for a living, leads, producers, senior and principal designers, and for those trying to get there. It is editorial. The reader comes for process, judgement and hard-won experience, not for a calculator. Its sister site gamesdesign.co.uk already does the self-serve side well: free client-side tools and structured guides on the mathematics of balance, for solo developers, students and small teams. gamedesign.uk must not duplicate any of that. It does not publish calculators, simulators, tool pages or a guide library, and where a piece would naturally point the reader at a tool it links to the one on gamesdesign.co.uk rather than building its own. The two sites should read as siblings that have divided the work between them, not as competitors. It must look visibly different from gamesdesign.co.uk, which is a dark site, near-black with cyan accents and a sans-serif face throughout: this one is light, a warm off-white paper ground with serif headings and a single muted earth-toned accent, the register of a practice journal rather than a tool dashboard. It is not a general design blog either, so no stark white minimalism and no hot accent colour. Its subject is game design and nothing wider. It is not a general design, web design or graphic design publication, and it does not stray into animation or cartoon work. Narrative design is in scope as a discipline of game design; general writing advice is not. Do not use the phrase game room or any variant of it. On money: there may in time be a paid offering for studios and design programmes. Nothing on the site should say one exists, name it, describe its features or price it, and nothing should rule one out either. Do not say the site is free, or that it does not sell anything, or otherwise define it by what it is not. Say what it is. Write for a working professional who has read a great deal of shallow material about game design and is tired of it. Short sentences, British English, no exclamation marks. Do not call anything powerful, seamless, effortless, essential or ultimate; describe what it does and let the reader judge. Do not state numbers of any kind, studio sizes, salaries, years of experience, reader counts, percentages, unless the owner has supplied them. Do not invent named studios, named people, case studies, job titles attributed to real organisations, or quotations. Where an opinion is given, give it as an opinion and say whose it is.
