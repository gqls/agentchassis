apis.uk is a personal page about bees, and that is the whole of it. The domain
name is the reason it exists: Apis is the genus of the honey bee, so a domain
called apis.uk ought to be about bees, and this one is. Treat that small joke as
the founding idea of the page and let it be quietly pleased with itself rather
than explaining itself at length.

The visitor to picture is a general reader who has arrived out of curiosity and
knows nothing in particular about bees. They are not a beekeeper, not a
biologist, and not looking for advice. They should leave having learned two or
three genuinely interesting things and feeling that whoever made this page finds
bees wonderful. That is the entire success condition.

Write it as one person writing plainly, in British English, in an unhurried and
warm voice. Not a brand. Not an institution. Not a campaign. Never breathless.
The register to aim for is a knowledgeable friend explaining something they love
over a cup of tea, and the highest compliment the page could earn is that
somebody read all of it without meaning to.

The subject matter is bees themselves and how they live, not how to keep them.
There is a great deal to choose from and the page should choose a few things and
do them properly rather than skim everything. Strong candidates: the way a
returning forager tells the rest of the colony where she has been by dancing on
the vertical comb, with direction read against gravity; the way wax is secreted
and worked into a structure that wastes nothing; the way a colony divides its
labour by age, so that a bee's job changes as she gets older; the drama and
strangeness of a swarm, which looks like catastrophe and is in fact reproduction;
and the fact that most bees are not honey bees at all but solitary insects that
most people walk past without ever noticing. There is also something quietly
worth saying about pollination being a side effect: the bee is going about its
own business entirely for its own reasons, and the rest of the living world
happens to depend on the by-product.

What this page is not, and these are firm. It sells nothing and collects nothing.
There is no product, no service, no consultancy, no newsletter, no signup, no
enquiry form and no call to action urging the visitor to do anything at all
beyond read and enjoy it. There are no testimonials, no client logos, no trust
badges and no statistics band. It is not a conservation campaign and should not
lecture, guilt or issue calls to arms, though it need not pretend everything is
fine either; where things are difficult it can say so plainly and without drama.
It is not a guide to keeping bees and should not instruct anyone to do anything
with a real colony.

Do not invent a personal history. Whether the owner keeps bees, has ever kept
bees, or simply likes them is not known and must not be assumed, implied or
filled in. Write about bees, not about an imagined beekeeper, and avoid any
sentence that quietly claims experience, credentials or years of anything.

One further constraint that matters more than it looks. This same domain also
runs a real and unrelated technical service on a different hostname. The page
must therefore never read as documentation, a developer reference, an endpoint
index or a product page for that service, and its imagery must never suggest
software, networks, circuits, dashboards or connected nodes. A single short,
plainly worded line somewhere unobtrusive, acknowledging that the domain also
hosts an unrelated technical service, is welcome so that a developer who lands
here by mistake is not left confused. One sentence, stated once, never dressed up
as a link to documentation and never allowed to compete with the page.

Finally, and this shapes every sentence: nothing about bees has been verified for
this site, so the page asserts no quantities of any kind. No counts, percentages,
distances, weights, temperatures, durations, lifespans or population trends,
whether written as digits or spelled out in words. This is not a real constraint
on how good the page can be, because none of what makes bees remarkable requires
a number. If a sentence seems to need one, rewrite the sentence so it does not,
and prefer a vivid plain description to a vague large quantity. Saying a colony
in high summer is crowded is better than reaching for a figure, and saying that
colony size swings so widely across the season that any single figure would
mislead is better still, and truer.
