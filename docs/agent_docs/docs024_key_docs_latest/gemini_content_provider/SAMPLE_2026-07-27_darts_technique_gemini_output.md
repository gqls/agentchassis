# Decoding the Pro Throw: Grip, Stance, and the Leap to a 100 Average

If you are playing at a club or county level, you already know the frustration of the plateau. You can hit 15-darters. You can take out 120 on the bull. On your best nights, you feel invincible. But then you watch the elite players on the professional tour, and you realize they are playing a fundamentally different game. They aren't just hitting the same shots you hit; they are doing it with a mechanical repeatability that defies logic. 

What actually separates the good from the great? It isn't magic, and it isn't just "natural talent." It comes down to the microscopic refinement of **darts technique**: the exact physics of the release, the kinetic chain of the body, the metronome of their rhythm, and a ruthless reduction of errors. 

Let’s look under the hood of the greatest players in the world to see how they actually throw, and what it takes to make the monumental leap from a 90 to a 100 average.

## The Three Main Grip Families (And How They Dictate Release)

There is no single "correct" **darts grip**. However, almost every elite player falls into one of three distinct grip families. Where you hold the barrel fundamentally changes whether you are *pushing* or *pulling* the dart, which in turn dictates the trajectory and entry angle into the board.

### 1. The Pencil Grip (The Push)
The most famous proponent of the pencil grip is undeniably Phil Taylor. In this grip, the dart rests on the thumb and is supported by the index and middle fingers, much like holding a pen. 
*   **The Mechanics:** This grip requires the player to push the dart from the center of gravity. 
*   **The Release:** Because it is a pushing motion, it generates less natural spin but incredible directional control. Players using a pencil grip often have a slightly lobbed trajectory, causing the dart to land tail-up in the board. This allows for "stacking"—using the first dart as a backboard to guide the next two into the treble bed.

### 2. The Front/Claw Grip (The Pull)
Look at Gary Anderson or Michael Smith. Their fingers are draped heavily toward the front of the barrel, near the point. 
*   **The Mechanics:** Instead of pushing the dart, front-grippers are effectively *pulling* the dart through the air. 
*   **The Release:** This pulling action naturally imparts more spin on the dart, acting like a gyroscope to stabilize it in flight. The result is a much flatter, more aggressive trajectory. The dart often lands flat or slightly tail-down, requiring the player to "under-stack" or switch targets if the bed gets crowded.

### 3. The Rear Grip (The Drive)
Players like Rob Cross grip the dart at the very rear of the barrel, sometimes with their fingers touching the stem.
*   **The Mechanics:** This is a pure driving motion. It requires a specific type of barrel (often longer and evenly weighted or front-loaded) to prevent the dart from fishtailing.
*   **The Release:** Rear-grippers must have an incredibly clean, late release. When executed correctly, it produces a heavy, forceful throw that powers through the air. Any snatching at the point of release, however, will cause the dart to kick wildly sideways.

## The Kinetic Chain: Stance and Elbow Alignment

Your **darts stance** is not just about where you put your feet; it is the foundation of your entire throwing plane. The way you stand dictates the alignment of your hips, which dictates your shoulders, which ultimately decides where your elbow sits. 

### Square (Side-On) Stance
Raymond van Barneveld is the textbook example of a square stance. His front foot is perfectly parallel to the oche, and his body is turned 90 degrees away from the board.
*   **Why it works:** By turning completely side-on, you lock the throwing shoulder in a forward position. This naturally forces the elbow into a perfect vertical plane with the target. There is very little lateral (side-to-side) movement available to the arm. If your elbow naturally flares out when you throw, adopting a more side-on stance will physically force it to tuck in.

### Front-Foot (Forward-Facing) Stance
Players like Michael van Gerwen and Adrian Lewis utilize a more forward-facing stance. Their front foot points toward the board (or at an angle), and they lean heavily over the oche.
*   **Why it works:** This stance provides incredible balance and allows the player to get their head physically closer to the board, reducing the throwing distance. 
*   **The Catch:** Because the shoulders are more open to the board, the elbow naturally wants to sit outside the line of the target. Players with this stance must have immense core stability and muscle memory to artificially pull the elbow across their body and keep it locked on a vertical hinge.

## Rhythm and Throw Tempo: The Unseen Engine

You can have a flawless grip and a perfectly aligned stance, but without a repeatable rhythm, your technique will collapse under pressure. Rhythm is what stops the brain from interfering with the body.

Consider the metronomic fluidity of Michael Smith. His tempo is rapid, but more importantly, it is *unbroken*. From the moment he steps to the oche, the draw back, the pause, and the release are all part of a single, continuous loop. A fast rhythm relies heavily on muscle memory and instinct.

Conversely, look at players with highly deliberate, measured tempos, like Gerwyn Price or Justin Pipe. They break the throw down into distinct mechanical phases. While slower, their rhythm is just as strict. 

**The Golden Rule of Tempo:** Your rhythm must dictate your throw, not the other way around. When club players get nervous, their draw-back speeds up, or they snatch at the release. Elite players maintain their exact tempo whether they are throwing for a 140 in the first leg or double 12 for a nine-darter.

## The Chasm: 90 vs. 100 Three Dart Average

At the county level, a 90 **three dart average** is exceptional. It means you are consistently throwing 16 or 17-dart legs. But stepping up to a 100 average—a 15-dart leg, every single time you step to the board—requires a monumental shift. 

What actually changes between a 90 and a 100 average? It is rarely about hitting more 180s. The difference lies in the floor, not the ceiling.

1.  **Eradicating the 1s and 5s:** A 90-average player will hit a treble 20, but if their dart sits at an awkward angle, their next dart might drift into the 5 or the 1. A 100-average player has the micro-control to adjust their trajectory by a millimeter, sliding the second dart past the flight of the first to rescue a 100 or 140. 
2.  **Grouping and Marker Darts:** Elite players do not aim at the treble 20 bed as a whole; they aim at the wire, or they aim at their first dart. If the first dart lands high in the treble, a 100-average player uses it as a physical backboard. Their grouping is so tight that the first dart dictates the exact flight path of the next two.
3.  **Setup Shots and Switching:** The 90-to-100 leap is highly mathematical. A 100-average player does not leave themselves 163 or 162. They switch to the 19s effortlessly to ensure they leave a finish (like 170 or 164). Furthermore, their setup darts to leave a preferred double (like Tops or Double 16) are ruthlessly precise. A 90-average player might leave 38; a 100-average player will almost always leave 40.
4.  **Consistency Under Pressure:** The mechanics do not degrade. When a 90-average player needs a treble to leave a double, their elbow might drop a fraction of an inch due to tension. The 100-average player's kinetic chain remains identical, whether it's practice or a tournament final.

## Conclusion: Taking It to the Practice Board

Improving your game isn't about copying Phil Taylor’s grip or Michael van Gerwen’s lean. It is about understanding *why* their techniques work and applying those mechanical truths to your own throw. 

Are your darts landing tail-down and blocking the bed? Look at your grip—you might be pulling the dart too much without enough loft. Are you missing wildly into the 5s and 1s? Check your stance—you may be too square-on, causing your elbow to flare out of alignment. 

**Your Call to Action:** Next time you are at the practice board, stop worrying about your score. Spend 30 minutes focusing purely on your mechanics. Film yourself from the side and from the front. Watch your elbow. Watch the angle of your dart as it hits the board. Find your natural rhythm, lock in your kinetic chain, and the averages will take care of themselves.
