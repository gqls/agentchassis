# SUMMARY — 2026-08-13 — the offer hardened, the brand landed, and a fleet defect got found

**What we're trying to do.** Sell AI-built websites with almost no human effort
per sale: a visitor talks to the chat box on webdesign.uk, a site gets built for
£149 all-in, they approve a preview, pay, and take the finished site away as a
ZIP to host wherever they like. Vouchers seed the first few customers; word of
mouth grows it.

**Where we've come from.** Yesterday morning the site still described a retired
offer. By the evening it sold £149 — and the migration that did it silently
deleted every call-to-action button on the site, which five green checks could
not see. Repairing that exposed the same damage on eighteen other sites.

**What we've done.** The owner hardened the offer: take it or leave it. No
changes included — the customer gets the finished files outright, and editing a
site that already works beats starting from a blank page; that argument is now
on the site, hard term first, ownership point second, no apology. No refund is
offered or mentioned; the two private remedies stay private. Third-party
services are named in the register for people who want to take the site
further — chosen for quality, not for commission, because the research showed
the best of them refuse affiliate schemes on principle.

The brand landed with it. One idea — a scrappy, unorthodox process producing a
genuinely good result — carried by Heath Robinson contraptions, a scruffy goose
with a golden egg, cardboard boxes and trade counters, in pen-and-ink on kraft
with a single gold accent reserved for the output. The rule that keeps it safe
is written where the image generator actually reads it: the cheapness is in the
service, never in the product. The first generated hero is live on the
homepage: an absurd pulley-and-counterweight machine delivering one immaculate
web page. The words stayed immaculate too — scrappy offer, never scruffy prose.

The button problem is solved on our site and filed for the fleet. Ours are
protected by component locks, proven by firing the exact rewrite that used to
delete them and watching it bounce off. The fleet defect — 214 dead
call-to-action buttons across 19 live sites, failing silently in every
instrument — is measured, reproduced with a control, and handed to another
thread with a cold-start brief that includes the two hypotheses already refuted
and the trap that refuted one of them: we repaired the evidence before asking
the diagnosis system to find it.

**Where we are now.** The site is accurate, on-brand at the homepage hero, and
its buttons cannot be deleted by automation. Payment stays keyless by design —
the site advertises an offer that cannot yet charge, which is the right way
round. One copy item is parked after three failed attempts: the FAQ still does
not name the six third-party services, and the third failure is unexplained —
the first two were our own spec defect, now fixed. A fresh platform build went
out this morning; the fleet button fix has not landed in it yet.

**Where we're going.** The owner looks at the new hero and says go, then the
remaining four heroes and the section icons follow, plus the site's overall
colours, which still belong to the old brand. The Stripe keys and the webhook
route remain the owner's two decisions between here and a first sale. Behind
them: the queue mechanism the copy now promises, automated ZIP delivery, and
the voucher screen. The fleet button fix proceeds in its own thread.
