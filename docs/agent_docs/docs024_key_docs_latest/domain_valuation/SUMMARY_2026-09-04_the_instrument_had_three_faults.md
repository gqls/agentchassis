# Domain valuation — where we are, 2026-09-04

*The instrument had three faults, all of them silent, and the day's work was
finding them rather than the valuations it was supposed to produce.*

---

## What we are trying to do

Put a defensible number against every domain the owner holds — 3,029 of them —
so he can sell roughly the bottom 500 at keen prices and keep the rest. The
numbers are an **input to a conversation about what to agree**, never a figure
that drops into a live price field. That distinction is his ruling, and today
made the case for it stronger rather than weaker.

## Where we have come from

A week ago the estate was not even countable: four registrars, no single list,
no categories, no idea which domains were still owned. By 2026-09-03 it was
counted, categorised into 32 categories and 170 sub-categories, and had a
working valuation model — and, importantly, a **finding about that model's
limits**. It cannot see a premium name. Asked to price `healthcare.uk`, which
cost £40,000, it produced $149 — the same figure it gave `healthcarecareers.uk`,
because both inherit the same category middle. Four guards were built to hold
that class of name out of automatic pricing altogether.

## What we have done

**Spent a full valuation window — 299 of the 300 daily calls — and took coverage
from 19% to 29%.** Every premium name in the estate is now valued, and every one
is still held back. But the substance of the day was three defects in the model,
each of which produced numbers that looked entirely reasonable.

**One: the guards switched themselves off.** Two of the four were written as
"hold this name back *if we have no valuation for it*". The premium queue exists
to go and get exactly those valuations — so doing the work the plan asked for
first disarmed the protection, name by name, with nothing to see in the output.
137 names lost their guard inside one window. Run to completion, 129 of the
estate's best domains would have entered the sell list carrying **$9.4 million**
of automatic asking prices. It had already been happening quietly for days:
`effectiveness.uk`, an ordinary English word, was sitting in the sell list at
**$350**. All four guards are now unconditional.

**Two: UK domains were discounted twice.** The model marks a .uk down to about a
fifth of a .com. Fifteen deliberate test valuations established that the
appraisal tool had *already* done that — it prices the actual domain, not the
keyword (`ant.uk` $23,144 against `ant.com` **$8.2 million**). So every UK domain
with its own valuation had been marked down roughly five times too far. Fixed.

**Three: names we refuse to price were setting everyone else's prices.** Fixing
the second fault exposed it. A category's middle was being computed from names
like `felines.co.uk` and `veterinary.co.uk` — valued in the hundreds of
thousands, and held out of the sell list precisely because the tool cannot
handle them. They were shaping the prices of their ordinary neighbours, pushing
an invented brandable to $6,250. Category middles are now built only from the
ordinary stock we would actually sell.

**Faults two and three are the same fault as the £40,000 one, in different
directions** — an average cannot tell a premium name from an ordinary one, so it
drags the best names down and, once the best names are in the pool, drags the
ordinary ones up.

Alongside: the appraisal queues are now rebuilt by a script rather than by hand
(the standing queue that morning still led with 95 domains from the category the
owner had ruled a permanent keep nine hours after that queue was written, and
carried all 23 of his withdrawn domains); the daily window runs under a lock,
after two copies of it briefly ran at once; and `mieleonline.com` and
`webuyanycarandvan.com` were withdrawn, closing the last trademark question.

## Where we are now

**872 of 3,023 domains valued (29%).** 2,429 sellable · 403 held as keeps · 184
held for the owner's own eye · 7 carrying his real figures.

The model is materially more trustworthy than yesterday, and — this is the part
worth saying plainly — **we now know three specific ways it was wrong, where
yesterday we knew one.** All three produced plausible numbers. None would have
been caught by reading the output.

One honest cost: excluding premium names from the category averages left about
400 domains resting on a weaker footing than they appeared to. That was always
true; it is now visible. 158 further valuations — half a window — repairs every
one of them.

**The provisional cut of the bottom 500 should not be used.** It rests on
yesterday's numbers, which carried all three faults.

## Where we are going

1. **Finish coverage.** About seven more daily windows. The next 158 calls are
   the highest-leverage: they re-anchor 555 domains.
2. **Then re-run the cut**, and only then — its shape is trustworthy at high
   coverage and not before.
3. **Keep asking him for real figures.** Every single one so far has overturned
   a model number by between 2× and 340×. One realised price is worth more than
   three hundred machine valuations, and that has not changed today — the model
   got better, and it is still the case that the four numbers we can check
   against reality are the only ones we know are right.
