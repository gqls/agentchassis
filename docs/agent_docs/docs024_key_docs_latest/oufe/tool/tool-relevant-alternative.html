The relevant alternative: who still has a vote

A restructuring plan can be imposed on a class that votes against it. Whether it can depends almost entirely on one assumption: what the court thinks would happen if the plan were refused. That assumption is called the relevant alternative. Move it, and classes gain or lose their ability to block.

Before you use this tool

This model can give you a wrong answer. It applies a simplified reading of one statutory test to numbers you type in. A real cram down turns on valuation evidence, how classes were composed, whether the court exercises its discretion at all, and much else that this models in no way whatsoever.

Treat every result as a possibly inaccurate worked example, useful for seeing how the mechanism behaves, and not as a view about any real company, any real plan or any real decision. Please do not rely on it.

What this does not model

It reads the two statutory conditions in a deliberately simple way. It treats a class as no worse off whenever its recovery under the plan is at least its recovery in the relevant alternative, measured only in money at a single point in time. The real test has been argued over timing, risk, the form of the consideration, and non-financial rights, and none of that appears here.

It also assumes the relevant alternative distributes value in the same strict order of priority as the plan, that classes were properly constituted, and that everyone entitled to vote did. It ignores security, guarantees, structural subordination, intercompany claims and contingent liabilities entirely. The court also retains a discretion it is not obliged to exercise, which no model can represent.

The 75% threshold and both conditions are quoted from Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006. The reading of them applied here is ours, and it is a simplification. It is educational scenario modelling, not a valuation, not a forecast and not advice.
