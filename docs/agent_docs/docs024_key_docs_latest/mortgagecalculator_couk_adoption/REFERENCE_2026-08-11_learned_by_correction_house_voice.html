Learned by correction

An evening spent rewriting one homepage, and what each correction turned out to be about. Almost none of it was about the writing model, and rather more of it than I would like was about rules I had written myself.

Look upstream of the writer before blaming the writer

The copy that started this had been commissioned, not merely written. An automatic review had gone looking for the homepage, failed to find it, and concluded that the site gave nobody a reason to choose it; the rewrite it ordered carried a pass mark of “claims a benefit other mortgage calculators do not have”, and named two competitors in the brief.

What arrived on the page was that instruction being obeyed exactly. Changing the model would have produced differently worded competitive copy, because the competitive claim was the acceptance test. When copy comes out wrong, the brief is the first place to look and the writer is the last.

Commissioned

See What the Bank’s Decision Engine Sees Before You Apply

There’s usually more to a mortgage than the rate

A voice is a written record, and it outlives everyone’s memory of writing it

The register nobody wanted was not an accident of generation. It was recorded in the site’s own settings at adoption, in as many words: be challenging rather than reassuring, never soften bad news, adopt the lender’s voice to sound like an insider, and put invented labels in quotation marks. Its own examples were “Flight Risk” and “The Inheritance Destroyer”. It also forbade a reassuring tone outright.

Every writer pointed at the site had been doing as it was told for weeks. A voice that lives in a document will be obeyed long after the reason for it is forgotten, so the document is the thing to change.

This is England

British copy runs to longer sentences and more complex grammar than American copy, and it favours understatement and a claim made once. The difference is structural rather than decorative: subordinate clauses are what make prose sound considered, and a house style that forbids them produces something that reads as impatient however warm the words are.

Americans like to be sold to; the British prefer to be persuaded.

Presumption is a matter of density, not of sentences

A heading that names the reader’s situation reads warmly enough on its own. What grates is the barrage, where every heading in turn tells the reader what they came for, what they are deciding and what to do next. The same sentence I had condemned as intrusive read perfectly well once the copy around it stopped doing the same thing.

So the rule is frequency, not prohibition: at most one such heading on a page, and rarely. Elsewhere, name the thing, observe something true, or use the inclusive conditional that covers the cases instead of choosing one of them for the reader.

Presuming the errand

The numbers you came to work out

Calculators for the parts that are hard to picture

Plain is not the opposite of clever

Told that the copy was trying too hard, I made it plain, and plain turned out to be a different target altogether. “Mortgage fee calculator” is informative, accurate and completely without character. The brief was never for less character; it was for character that serves the reader rather than the writer.

Say what a thing is

A negative definition asks the reader to do subtraction, and it reads colder because it withholds. Naming the thing directly is both warmer and more informative, and it usually costs no more words.

Defined by what it is not

the guides go into the parts that aren’t just arithmetic

the guides go into the parts that are more judgement than arithmetic

A contraction keeps the company of its neighbours

Contractions make ordinary sentences friendlier, which is why my rule had simply preferred them. That rule was too blanket. An uncommon or formal word lifts the register of its clause, and a casual contraction beside it jars: are not just arithmetic is right where aren’t just arithmetic is wrong, because arithmetic is not an everyday word.

Either use the everyday word and contract freely, or keep the less common word and write the full form. What does not work is mixing the two inside one clause.

Read it aloud

This is the test that beats any list of banned words. Both phrases below are grammatical, on-message and perfectly clear, and neither is a thing a person says out loud. No vocabulary rule would have caught either; reading them aloud catches both immediately.

Nobody says this

the questions a single number can’t settle

nothing here is selling you a deal

Everything’s free to use, and you don’t need to sign up for any of it.

Do not borrow a term you cannot pay for

Nobody outside the industry calls it a lender’s decision engine. Borrowing a term like that has to buy something — a joke, an angle, a genuine piece of understanding — and where it buys nothing it simply sounds technical at a reader who came for an answer about their own money.

A rule is a prompt for judgement, not a substitute for it

Three times in one evening I turned a sound observation into a hard rule, and then let the rule write the copy. A borrowed sentence-length ceiling produced the staccato. An outright ban on presumption produced flatness. A list of forbidden phrases had me reporting four perfectly good card descriptions as defects, on no better grounds than that they matched my own list.

Written rules are still worth having, because they are what stops a voice drifting back. They earn that place by being written as guidance with a reason attached, rather than as prohibitions a later reader will apply mechanically.

Provenance is not fitness

The staccato came from thresholds I had imported with some confidence: measured, dated, owner-directed, and shipped that same morning by a capable team. They were also drawn from Simplified Technical English, which exists to make safety-critical maintenance instructions unambiguous for non-native readers. Correct, well-sourced, and inapplicable.

Naming the corpus a borrowed number was measured on takes one sentence, and that sentence is usually enough. “Safety instructions for non-native readers” does not survive being set beside “warm copy for British homebuyers”.

The page is the only test

Every correction above was caught by reading the live page, and not one was caught by the rules, the checks or the specification — all of which I had written from the same premises that produced the faults. A style guide cannot test itself, and copy that passes every check you thought to write is only evidence about what you thought to check.

Where it ended: There’s usually more to a mortgage than the rate — which says something true, presumes nothing about why anyone arrived, and leads on more than the price, because the price is rarely all that is being decided.
