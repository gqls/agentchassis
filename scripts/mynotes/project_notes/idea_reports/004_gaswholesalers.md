Your idea report

This report is from idea.uk. You asked us to find AI product ideas for gaswholesalers.com. We came up with a wide range of ideas, then checked each one against what already exists and whether people would actually pay for it. Below are the ideas worth pursuing — with what we found and a cheap way to test each one — followed by the ideas we looked at and set aside, and why.
Who it's for

People whose job is to buy or sell large volumes of natural gas and oil for a living, plus the senior bosses who run gas and oil companies (the top boss, the head o f technology, and the head of computer systems).
Why they'd pay

These people move huge amounts of money on small price differences, so even a tiny edge or a saved hour is worth a great deal to them, and they already pay heavily for data feeds, news, and analysis. The risk is that the most senior bosses rarely sit and read a website themselves; they have staff do it, so a plain information site may not earn money from them directly.
Ideas worth pursuing (best first)
1. Deal contract checker
   worth considering

Heads-up: needs legal and insurance groundwork before building (risk 2/5 — see below).

A free model has never seen your firm's own signed deals, so it cannot tell you 'this payment term is tighter than the last twelve cargoes you bought'.

What it's built on: The firm's own library of past gas and oil sale and purchase contracts, plus the standard industry master agreements they trade under, using An AI that reads a new draft contract against your own past deals and flags clauses that differ from what you usually accept.

What we found: I checked that the standard industry agreements are real and that firms actually pay for contract-checking software. The main European standard for gas trading (the EFET General Agreement) is real and in wide use, and contract-review tools sell for roughly 500 to 50,000 dollars a year, so people do pay. The tools that exist are general legal checkers, not built around a gas trader's own past signed deals, so the firm's private contract library is a genuine edge.

How it scored: out of 5 — hard to copy 4 · people will pay 4 · easy to build 3 · reusable elsewhere 3 · built to last 4 (18 out of 25 overall)

Risk to you: 2/5 (high — needs a person to check every report, insurance, and carefully checked terms before building)

A cheap first test: Validate demand first; do not build until PII insurance is in force and T&Cs are reviewed by a UK solicitor. Show a one-page description of the tool to three or four contract managers at gas trading firms and ask whether they would pay a named monthly fee for it, and what they currently use instead.
2. Pipeline notice reader
   wo rth testing now

A free model will not log into and watch dozens of operator portals all day; this one fetches the notices and ties them to your specific deals.

What it's built on: The maintenance and capacity notices that pipeline and storage operators publish on their own websites and portals, using An AI that watches those operator pages, reads each notice, and tells you in plain words which of your routes or storage sites are affected and by how much.

What we found: I checked that operators really publish maintenance and capacity-cut notices in one place, and they do: European gas networks are required to post these 'urgent market messages' on public sites (the ENTSOG transparency platform and the REMIT inside-information sites), and re ading them is free. Tying each notice to the firm's own routes, storage and deals is the part no one else does for you.

How it scored: out of 5 — hard to copy 3 · people will pay 4 · easy to build 3 · reusable elsewhere 3 · built to last 4 (17 out of 25 overall)

Risk to you: 3/5 (moderate — show your sources; insurance for handling personal data is recommended)

A cheap first test: Put up a one-page description and a sign-up form, then call five gas traders directly and ask whether they would pay a specific monthly price to get every capacity or maintenance notice matched to their own routes within minutes of it being published.
3. Boss morning one-pager
   worth testing now

It is built from the firm's own private book and feeds, so the boss gets a ready answer, not a generic market comment a free model would give.

What it's built on: The firm's positions, the day's market moves, and the news feeds, using An AI that delivers a finished one-page note to a senior boss each morning: where money was made or lost, the biggest risk today, one thing to decide.

What we found: The things this relies on, the firm's own positions, the day's price moves and its news feeds, are already held inside the firm, so the asset is real and private. General AI report writers exist but cannot write from your private book, which is exactly the edge; I could not fully confirm a set price people pay for a daily boss note, but firms in this trade already pay for market data and reporting.

How it scored: out of 5 — hard to copy 4 · people will pay 3 · easy to build 3 · reusable elsewhere 3 · built to last 4 (17 out of 25 overall)

Risk to you: 3/5 (moderate — show your sources; insurance for handling personal data is recommended)

A cheap first test: Mock up one example one-pager using dummy position and price data, show it to three trading-firm heads or chief risk officers, and ask whether they would pay a named monthly fee to receive this every morning built from their own live book.
